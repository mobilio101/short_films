# THE DRAGON JAR: Short plan (draft, not animated yet)

- Topic chosen from the most-viewed Short on a reference channel ("The Ancient Jar That Warned an
  Empire", 34M views). Same historical subject, but our own research, script and visuals; nothing
  copied from that video.
- Format: YouTube Short, 1080×1920, 60 seconds (1800 frames at 30 fps)
- Language and voice: English, ElevenLabs Brian (`eleven_v3`), same narrator as the Ghost Army Short
- Style: Newsprint (cream paper, black ink, one red). No shadow character.
- Title options: "The Jar That Felt an Earthquake No One Felt" (first choice),
  "The 2,000-Year-Old Earthquake Detector", "Why This Dragon Jar Dropped a Ball"
- End card: **THE DRAGON JAR.**

## Facts (checked online)

| Fact | Used in script as | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Zhang Heng presented his device to the Han court in AD 132. Its name, Houfeng Didong Yi, is usually translated "instrument for measuring the seasonal winds and the movements of the Earth". | "In the year one hundred thirty-two… presented China's emperor with a bronze jar" | Amusing Planet, HeritageDaily |
| A bronze vessel shaped like a wine jar, with eight dragon heads facing the eight directions, each holding a ball, and a toad with open mouth below each one. In a quake, one ball dropped into a toad, showing the direction. | dragons, balls, toads, direction | Amusing Planet, SCMP |
| A ball dropped though no one in the capital felt a quake; scholars at court doubted it. Days later a messenger reported a strong quake in the direction shown. | "nobody felt a thing… the court doubted it… days later, a messenger" | Amusing Planet, HeritageDaily |
| The quake was to the northwest, in the Longxi/Gansu region, several hundred kilometres from the capital, Luoyang. | "northwest, hundreds of kilometers away" | Ancient Pages, HeritageDaily, ProQuest study |
| The original device is lost and its inner mechanism is unknown; replicas have been built (one by the Chinese Academy of Sciences in 2005), and Chinese scientists were still working on a new reconstruction in 2025. | "The original was lost… scientists are still trying to rebuild it" | Engadget, SCMP (2025) |
| It is widely described as the first known seismoscope (earthquake detector). | "the world's first known earthquake detector" | HeritageDaily, Engadget |

Disagreements, and how the script handles them: the date of the detected quake is given as AD 138
traditionally, but one study argues for AD 134, so the script gives no year for it. The distance is
given as anything from about 500 to 850 km, so the script says "hundreds of kilometers". Some scholars
doubt parts of the story; the script says "first known", not "first ever".

## Voiceover script (v4, emotional roller coaster)

Nobody felt the earthquake. Except a bronze jar.
China, the second century. Zhang Heng stands before the emperor's court with a machine no one has ever
seen. Eight bronze dragons. Eight balls. Eight toads, mouths open, waiting. He makes a bold promise: it
will feel earthquakes no one else can.
Then, one silent day… clang. A single ball drops.
Everyone freezes. They wait. Nothing. Not a tremor.
Then the laughing starts. A toy! A fraud! The scholars mock him. His enemies at court smell blood. And
Zhang Heng is thrown out of the palace, his name a joke.
One day passes. Then another.
Then hooves thunder at the gates. A messenger, covered in dust. A massive earthquake has struck the
northwest, hundreds of kilometers away. Exactly where the dragon pointed.
The laughing stops.
And the strangest part? The jar was lost. Two thousand years later, no one knows how it worked.
So how did a jar feel what no one could?

Emotional curve: hook → wonder and a bold promise (up) → the clang (shock) → nothing (doubt, down) →
mockery and expulsion (humiliation, lower) → the wait (lowest) → the messenger (vindication, way up) →
"The laughing stops." (payoff) → lost and unexplained (twist) → question loops to the hook.
Dramatic licence (agreed with the user): the mockery, "A toy! A fraud!" and being thrown out of the
palace are dramatised. On record: the capital's scholars blamed the device when no quake was felt, and
Zhang Heng's enemies at court (palace eunuchs) pushed him out of the capital around AD 136.
